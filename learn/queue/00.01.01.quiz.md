## Q1 A patient as a vector, a panel as a matrix
A screening panel holds 12 test results for each of 2,000 patients, stored as a matrix `X`. What is the shape of `X`, what does one row stand for, and what does one column stand for? If you multiply `X` by a weight vector `w` with one weight per test, what shape is `X @ w`, and what does each entry mean clinically?
- H1: Picture the results spreadsheet: what goes down the side and what goes across the top?
- H2: (patients × tests) times (tests) leaves one number per patient.

## Q2 Dot product, cosine and distance
On two tests, patient A has results (2.0, 0.8) and patient C has (0.8, 0.32), both in SD units. Work out cos(A, C) and the straight-line distance between them. What does that pair of answers tell you about what cosine ignores and distance does not?
- H1: Is C just a shorter copy of A?
- H2: C = 0.4 × A. Cosine divides the lengths out; the distance is ‖A − C‖ = 0.6 × ‖A‖, with ‖A‖ = √(2.0² + 0.8²).

## Q3 Units decide who is "similar"
A colleague builds a "patients like this one" tool from age in years plus the 12 tests in SD units, and does not standardise. What happened to the nearest neighbours and to the held-out AUC in the notebook, and why? When you do standardise, which patients must the mean and SD be learned from?
- H1: Which column has by far the biggest spread of numbers?
- H2: A squared distance adds (age gap)² in years² to (test gap)² in SD², so a 10-year gap counts as much as a 10-SD gap. Rescaling a column is x ↦ Dx, which changes the inner product to xᵀD²y.

## Q4 Redundant tests: rank and condition number
Two tests on the panel are near-copies, and the noise separating them shrinks to zero. Say what happens to (a) the singular values, (b) the condition number, (c) the least-squares coefficients of the two tests and (d) the held-out AUC of the linear score `Xw`. Which way of solving failed silently?
- H1: The data can pin down only one combination of the two tests' weights.
- H2: The smallest singular value shrinks in proportion to the noise σ, so κ grows like 1/σ. Only w₀₁ + w₁₂ is fixed, and the normal equations square κ.

## Q5 From similarity to a prediction, and its limits
The cosine 25-NN vote scores a held-out AUC of 0.919. At the clinician two-test rule's specificity (0.67) its sensitivity is 0.93, against the rule's 0.90. Can you say the vote is better than the rule? What does a score of 20/25 mean for the patient in front of you? And what happened when the panel was padded with 300 uninformative tests?
- H1: How many diseased patients were in the test set, and how wide was the bootstrap range of the AUC?
- H2: There were 92 diseased new patients, so 0.03 × 92 ≈ 3 patients; the bootstrap range was 0.88–0.95.

---
<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. `X` is 2,000 × 12. A row is one patient's whole panel: the patient as a vector. A column is one test across all patients. `X @ w` has shape (2,000,): one linear risk score per patient, a weighted sum of that patient's results. Under a `predict` goal the weights are not mechanisms.
2. cos(A, C) = 1.00, because C points exactly the way A does. The distance is 0.6 × √4.64 ≈ 1.29. Cosine says "same pattern"; distance says "C is much milder". Cosine ignores severity (length), and distance and the dot product keep it.
3. Age (SD ≈ 14) outweighs every test (SD ≈ 1), so the neighbours become age-matched rather than disease-matched. Only 21% of neighbours were the same as under standardisation, and the AUC fell from 0.924 to 0.898. Stretching age ×100 dropped it to 0.72. The units act as hidden weights. Learn the mean and SD from the seen (training) patients only and apply them to new patients; fitting on everyone leaks the new patients into their own units.
4. (a) One singular value collapses (2.3 against 34–54 for the rest). Its direction is bm_12 − bm_01, the pair the generator made collinear. (b) κ climbs tenfold per tenfold drop in noise: 206 at 0.01, 2,064 at 10⁻³, about 1.4 × 10¹⁶ at an exact copy, where the rank drops from 12 to 11. (c) The two coefficients run off in opposite directions, ±90,480 at σ = 10⁻⁸, while their sum stays at 0.144. (d) The AUC stays at 0.935 throughout: redundancy ruins the explanation, not the prediction. The normal equations (`np.linalg.solve` on XᵀX) failed silently: wrong by a factor of ten at σ = 10⁻⁸, and at σ = 0 they returned an arbitrary split (0.020, 0.125) with no error. Use `lstsq` (SVD) or QR.
5. No. The difference is about three of 92 diseased patients, inside the resampling spread (bootstrap 0.88–0.95), so the vote matches the rule. Its real gain is that its threshold can move. A score of 20/25 means 20 of the 25 most similar past patients had the condition. That is a statement about look-alikes, not a calibrated probability or a diagnosis, and these results are retrospective and synthetic; the bar is a prospective evaluation. Padding with 300 uninformative tests dropped the cosine vote from 0.930 to 0.792 and the Euclidean vote from 0.916 to 0.735. Distances concentrated too: the gap between the nearest and farthest seen patient fell from 3.1× to 0.26× the nearest distance.
