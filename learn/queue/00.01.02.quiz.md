## Q1 What an eigenvector is
A matrix A turns the plane: most arrows come out pointing somewhere new. Describe in your own words what is special about an eigenvector of A, and what its eigenvalue tells you. For a covariance matrix of a patient panel, what do the two mean clinically?
- H1: Think about which arrows are not turned.
- H2: Av = λv: the arrow keeps its line and is multiplied by λ. For a covariance, v is a combination of tests and λ is the variance of patients along it.

## Q2 Power iteration and deflation
You multiply a random vector by the covariance S over and over, rescaling to length 1 each time. Which direction does it converge to, why, and how do you get the second direction? In the notebook the first eigenvalue took 12 steps and the later ones over 130: why the difference?
- H1: Which direction does S stretch the most on every pass?
- H2: It converges to the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue; subtract λ₁v₁v₁ᵀ and repeat. Speed depends on the ratio of neighbouring eigenvalues (about 1.12/2.11 for the first, about 1.07/1.12 for later ones).

## Q3 Reading a panel's eigenvalues
Twelve standardised tests give eigenvalues that sum to 12. One is 2.11, ten lie between 0.82 and 1.12, and one is 0.004. What do the 2.11 and the 0.004 each tell you about the panel, and what would the loadings of the 0.004 direction look like?
- H1: What would two tests that are copies of each other do to the variance along their sum and along their difference?
- H2: λ ≈ 1 ± ρ for two tests with correlation ρ. The 0.004 direction is "test A minus test B", with loadings about ±0.707.

## Q4 Rotation versus rescaling
You rotate all twelve tests by an orthogonal matrix Q. Separately, you multiply one test by 3. What happens to the eigenvalues and eigenvectors in each case, and why does it matter for choosing units?
- H1: Does a rotation change how spread out the cloud is?
- H2: Rotation: eigenvalues identical, eigenvectors become Qᵀv. Rescaling is not orthogonal, so eigenvalues change (top 2.11 → 9.14 in the notebook). Choosing units is choosing a rescaling.

## Q5 When the decomposition misleads
Give two ways, from the notebook, in which eigenvectors can mislead, and say why "the biggest eigenvalue carries the disease" is not guaranteed even though it did here.
- H1: One concerns units, one concerns sample size.
- H2: Age in years dominates the top direction when not standardised; directions with crowded eigenvalues (0.82–1.12) are not reproducible on resampling (mean |cos| 0.46). The decomposition never sees the diagnosis; it predicted well here only because the duplicated pair includes a strong test, which is a fact about this panel.

---
<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. An eigenvector is a direction that a matrix only stretches (or shrinks, or flips), never turns; the eigenvalue is the stretch factor. For a covariance, the eigenvector is a combination of tests and the eigenvalue is the variance of the patients along it.
2. It converges to the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue, because each multiplication stretches that component most. Deflation (S − λ₁v₁v₁ᵀ) removes it and the next largest takes over. Convergence rate is governed by λ₂/λ₁: far apart is fast (12 steps), crowded is slow (over 130).
3. 2.11 means one direction is much wider than a single test would give: here the duplicated test adds to itself. 0.004 means a combination on which patients barely differ: one test is a near-copy of another. Its loadings are about +0.707 and −0.707 on the duplicated pair.
4. Rotation leaves eigenvalues exactly unchanged and rotates the eigenvectors with the data. Rescaling one test changes eigenvalues and eigenvectors. Hence standardise (on training patients) before interpreting.
5. Unstandardised age in years made the top eigenvector entirely age. Too few patients, or eigenvalues close together, make directions unstable. The largest direction is not necessarily the disease; the decomposition ignores the outcome, and it predicted well here only because the duplicated pair includes a strong test.
